About this site

This site is an interactive guide to the probability distributions that are most often used in statistics and in computational modeling, and to the ways in which they are related to one another. It is meant for students and researchers who want to see how a distribution changes as its parameters change, to look up its formulas and properties, and to understand where it comes from and what it is used for.

It covers 32 distributions and 58 relationships between them. Fifteen of the distributions come from a widely used chart of distribution relationships in the style of Leemis and McQueston (2008); the others were added because they are common in frequentist statistics (the F, Laplace and logistic distributions, for example), in Bayesian statistics (the beta-binomial, inverse-gamma, half-Cauchy and Dirichlet distributions) and in computational modeling of behavior and the brain (the von Mises, ex-Gaussian and Wald distributions). The list of distributions groups them by family, and the list of relationships groups the relationships by kind.

What is on the site

  • The map (the home page) shows the distributions as tiles and their relationships as arrows. Clicking a tile opens the page for that distribution. Clicking an arrow or its label opens a short explanation of the relationship, with a link to a page where you can explore it.
  • Distribution pages all have the same sections: a short description; an interactive plot of the density (or, for counts, the probability mass function) next to the cumulative distribution function; the formulas, with every symbol explained; a table of properties such as the mean, variance and skewness; limits and special cases; uses; related distributions; and references.
  • Uses are listed in three groups. Frequentist statistics: the tests, models and estimators that use the distribution. Bayesian statistics: conjugate priors and their updates, typical priors and posteriors, and predictive distributions. Computational modeling and other applications: for example response-time models, choice models, simulation and reliability.
  • Relationship pages explain one connection between two distributions, such as a special case, a limit, a transformation or a conjugate prior. Each has a plain explanation, the formal statement, a glossary of the symbols, an interactive demonstration and a short explanation of why the relationship holds.

How to use the interactive plots

  • Handles that you can drag are drawn in amber. A dashed vertical line usually sets a location such as the mean; a horizontal arrow usually sets a scale such as the standard deviation. Curves are drawn in blue.
  • Each parameter also has a number box. Dragging a handle updates the box, and typing a value in the box updates the plot. If a value is outside the allowed range, a short message explains what is allowed and the plot keeps the last valid value.
  • A handle can also be selected with the Tab key and moved with the arrow keys (hold Shift for larger steps).
  • The area under the curve switch shades the area to the left or right of a point, or between two points, and shows its value. That area is a probability. The same number appears on the cumulative distribution plot as the height of the curve at that point (or one minus that height for the area to the right), which shows how the density and the CDF are connected. For a count, the switch highlights the bars instead and adds up their probabilities.
  • The properties table has a column of values computed for the parameters currently set in the plot, so it changes as you drag. Entropies are given in nats (natural logarithms).
  • On a relationship page, the demonstration either simulates random draws and compares them with the distribution the relationship predicts, or draws two distributions on top of each other so you can watch them approach each other as a parameter moves toward its limit. In both cases the largest gap between the two cumulative distribution functions is reported.

How it is built

The site is a set of static pages with no server-side code. Plots are drawn as SVG with a small plotting library written for the site, and all probabilities, quantiles and special functions are computed in the browser. Formulas are typeset with KaTeX. Automated tests check every distribution numerically: that each density integrates to 1, that the cumulative distribution and quantile functions agree with it, and that the mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis, entropy, median and mode in the properties table match values computed by numerical integration.

The pages were drafted with the help of an AI assistant (Claude, by Anthropic) and checked against the textbooks above and with the numerical tests. The source code is on GitHub; corrections and suggestions are welcome as issues there.
